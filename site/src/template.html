Jackintosh

Switch on your Atari. A Macintosh Plus comes up.

The genuine Mac Plus ROM runs natively on the Atari's own 68000: the happy Mac, System 6, the Finder and the Mac software of 1986 to 1990, from virtual floppies and hard disks on a microSD card. No TOS, no GEM, no emulator program: the Atari is the Mac from the moment you power it on.

Why “Jackintosh”?

In 1985 Jack Tramiel, who had just bought Atari, launched the Atari ST: a 68000, a mouse, a windowed desktop and a sharp monochrome screen, at a fraction of the Macintosh's price. The press called it the “Jackintosh”.

Forty years later the joke comes true: with the right ROM in its sockets, the Atari ST boots as a Macintosh Plus. Jack is watching.

What you get

  • The genuine Mac Plus ROM (v3), patched in place: the Mac's own code, at full Atari speed.
  • 640×400 on a monochrome monitor, or the classic 512×342 window.
  • Up to 4 MB of Mac RAM.
  • Floppies and a hard disk from the Multi-device's microSD card, swapped from a menu at boot.
  • The clock from the network (NTP) and the Mac's PRAM saved on the card.
  • Mac sound on the STE and Mega STE; the Mega STE at 16 MHz with its cache.
  • Your Atari keyboard: US, French, German, Spanish and UK layouts.

What you need

  • An Atari ST, STF, STFM, Mega ST, STE or Mega STE with a monochrome monitor (an SM124, or any mono display).
  • The SidecarTridge TOS Emulator in the TOS ROM sockets: it serves the Jackintosh image as the Atari's ROM.
  • The SidecarTridge Multi-device in the cartridge port, running the md-jackintosh microfirmware: floppies, hard disk, clock and PRAM.
  • Your own Mac Plus ROM (the v3 ROM, 128 KB): Apple's code is never distributed here.

5. System 6's absolute addresses

System 6.0.8 fixes bugs in the Plus ROM with its own code, and that code calls the ROM at absolute addresses: $40xxxx, where a Mac Plus has its ROM. On an Atari the ROM is elsewhere. So as each System resource loads, the glue checks it (type, ID, size, SHA-256) and moves every listed address to where the ROM really is.

6. Interrupts and time

The MFP's Timer C: 2,457,600 Hz / 200 / 204 = 60.235 Hz, a tick that steps by exactly one (the Mac's window dragging waits for an exact value). Over a minute it gains about 5 ticks on a real Plus.

7. The Mac's chips, in RAM

The Mac ROM reaches most of its chips through pointers in low memory. The glue points them at small copies in RAM and plays the chips' part from there: faster and simpler than catching every access.

8. The bus-error handler

Mac programs write through nil pointers all the time: on a Mac that lands in RAM at address 0 and nobody notices. On an Atari, address 0 is ROM and the write is a bus error. The glue's handler finds the instruction and skips it. Step through an example (our own code, not Apple's).

  MOVEA.L  handle,A0     ; A0 = 0 (no memory)  MOVE.L   D0,4(A0)      ; write to $000004  ADDQ.W   #1,D1  RTS

9. Keyboard and mouse

The Atari's keyboard has its own processor, the IKBD, which sends key presses and mouse moves as bytes. The glue turns them into what a Mac Plus keyboard would send, and plays that keyboard's half of the Mac's serial conversation every tick. Point at a key.

10. Video

A Mac Plus draws 512x342 pixels; the Atari's monochrome mode shows 640x400. The Mac ROM is told the bigger screen, so the desktop grows. Or, if a program insists on a Plus screen, keep 512x342 framed in black. Drawn to scale.

11. The Multi-device

The cartridge port can only be read by the Atari. So to send a word, the Atari reads an address that encodes it: $FB0000 plus the word. The Multi-device's RP2040 watches the addresses and answers by writing into a 64 KB window the Atari reads at $FA0000. Step through a disk read.
